# Concept and hook

Start with the creator's seed, audience, length and strongest concrete detail. Name the immediate desire and the circumstance that makes the ordinary way of getting it expensive. Let the reader understand the situation before they learn its proper nouns.

1. If invention is open, try three materially different engines, such as an impossible job, a social obligation, and a private rule the lead must break. Change who must choose and what the choice costs. When the premise is locked, vary the point of entry instead.
2. For each viable route, write the cover promise in one breath, the first image as marks an artist can put on paper, and one later action that delivers the promise. A striking cover image earns its place when the same tension changes a scene.
3. Test the opening twice. Hide the title and ask what a newcomer could infer from the first unit. Then substitute a generic protagonist. If the same opening still works unchanged, find the behavior only this lead would attempt. A quiet opening can hook through intimacy, an odd ritual, or a precise discrepancy.
4. For a serial, invent a second problem that the mechanism could generate without a larger villain. For a one-shot, identify a decision that can exhaust the premise inside the page budget.

“A city where shadows can be taxed” suggests a setting. A child folds herself into her mother's shadow at the collection booth so they can afford the fare home. Now the artist has a first panel, and the collector's next move matters.

**Deliver** the selected logline, the opening panel or strip, the question it creates, and a scene that pays for the attention. If comparing options, say what makes one better suited to this reader and length. Use the [brief and hook sheet](../../templates/brief-and-hook.md) when a project needs a record.
